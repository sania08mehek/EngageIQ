Abstract—Remote  work  has  stripped  managers  of  cues  that 
they  used  to  identify  when  a  team  member  was  under  stress. 
And in response to this challenge, may organizations have chosen 
keystroke  tracking,  screens-and-webcam  monitoring  solutions 
that  destroy  trust.  In  this  paper,  we  introduce  EngageIQ  –  an 
analytics platform that has been developed on top of PACE score, 
which  looks  at  work  from  two  perspectives  rather  than  one.  A 
Signal  Layer  captures  how  things  happen  with  only  metadata 
(calendar  data  and  comms)  and  the  Output  Layer  summarizes 
the  results  produced  by  the  metadata  (tasks  and  code  repos) 
of  completion  rates,  velocities,  adherence  to  deadlines,  and  re-
work. Rating indices EHS and OPI are fused with weights of 0.4 
and  0.6  respectively  so  quiet  collaborators  are  not  punished  for 
their  collaboration  habits.  Rolling  baseline  deviation  and  three-
band  risk  classifier  make  scoring  a  human-interpretable  signal, 
and  all  cases  of  alert  are  forwarded  to  human  analysts  and 
not  automatically  escalated.  The  system  is  coded  with  FastAPI, 
SQLalchemy backend and React and TypeScript dashboard. On 
synthetic data set with 50 employees and seven teams, the pipeline 
has classified 15 employees as healthy, 25 as moderate, and 10 as 
At-Risk, storing EHS/OPI breakdown behind each classification. 

Index  Terms—employee  engagement,  burnout  detection,  re-
sponsible  AI,  workplace  analytics,  privacy  by  design,  anomaly

I.  INTRODUCTION 

When  a  team  leaves  the  shared  office,  a  manager  looses 
plenty  of  invisible  information.  Nobody  sees  who  stays  late 
three days in row, who stops joining the discussions or whose 
day  is  full  of  meetings  and  nobody  can  think.  Invisible 
in  this  way,  burnouts  which  are  defined  by  World  Health 
Organization  as  an  occupational  phenomenon  that  emerges 
due  to  unmanageable  chronic  stress  [2]  harm  well-being  and 
performance,  and  this  fact  is  well  proven  by  the  scientific 
literature [1]. 

The  natural  reaction  of  organizations  is  to  control  even 
more.  Keystroke  loggers,  making  screenshots  and  tracking 
"active" status is available and justified easily to the manager. 
But  these  are  bad  indicators  of  productive  work.  Reviews  of 
monitoring in electronic work places indicate that monitoring 
can result in stress and increase of autonomy [8]. Furthermore, 
religious  limits  still  exist,  since  GDPR  and  other  law  require 
personal data to be collected for specific purposes only [3]. 

EngageIQ  asks  different  question.  Instead  of  checking  if 
someone is working at the keyboard, it asks whether the work 
pattern is healthy and whether effectiveness is sustainable and 
treats  the  results  just  as  a  basis  for  dialogue.  This  model 
is  based  on  PACE  model  which  applies  dual  lens  paradigm. 
The  Signal  Layer  characterizes  work  pattern  based  only  on 
metadata  of  calendars  and  collaborative  tools.  The  Output 
Layer shows output basing only on metadata that comes from 
task trackers and code repositories. The results are summarized 
in one score but output weights in this sum heavier. 

The roles of the papers are the following: 
•  A  dual  lens  score  model  called  PACE  model  evaluates 
separation  of  work  pattern  development  (EHS)  from 
output  delivery  (OPI)  and  merges  two  parameters  into 
one score (40% to 60%). 

•  A  lightweight  and  interpretable  trend  and  risk  pipeline 
comparing  individual  with  historical  baseline  of  their 
scores and preserving the EHS and OPI decomposition. 
•  A proof-of-concept implementation in a FastAPI service, 
relational  store  and  React  dashboard  with  role-based 
views,  plus  an  interactive  simulator  that  lets  the  user 
explore how their readings contribute to the score. 

•  Discussion of the privacy, fairness and human-oversight 
constraints informing the design, together with an honest 
discussion of the limits of the current evaluation.

II.  RELATED  WORK 

A.  Burnout and Work Design 

Maslach  and  Leiter  define  burnout  as  a  combination  of 
exhaustion,  cynicism  and  diminished  efficacy,  and  associate 
it  with  mismatch between  individuals  and  their  jobs  in  terms 
of  excessive  workload,  lack  of  control  and  weak  recognition 
[1]. The Job Demands–Resources framework provides another 
view:  high  demand  levels  without  adequate  resources  will 
cause strain and disengagement [9]. These literatures agree that 
load and autonomy are important. The broader people analytics 
field, which Polzer reviews, has rapidly expanded within orga-
nizations, and also raises ethical questions about privacy and 
inference from organizational research [13]. Machine learning 
research on workforce outcomes, exemplified by the review of 
employee turnover prediction by Talebi et al. [12], reveals how 
much  of  this  literature  depends  on  human-resources  records 
and surveys instead of everyday work metadata. That explains 
the  choice  of  load,  uninterrupted  focus  time  and  after-hours 
work  as  the  main  inputs  in  the  Signal  Layer,  since  each  of 
these  is  roughly  equivalent  to  a  demand  indicator  or  lack  of 
control over one’s time. 

B.  Measures of Productivity 

Forsgren  et  al.  claim  that  there  is  no  single  metric  that 
can  adequately  capture  developer  productivity  and  suggest 
analysing several dimensions: satisfaction, activity, communi-
cations and efficiency [10]. Similarly, EngageIQ does not trust 
single-number measures of either engagement or productivity, 
presents both lenses separately in the UI, shows the individual 
components and uses their combination mostly as a pointer to 
where the person needs to focus first. 

C.  Workplace Monitoring and Responsible AI 

Ball’s literature review for the European Commission covers 
the spread of workplace monitoring and the associated policy 
issues  such  as  proportionality,  transparency  and  participation 
[8]. In the responsible AI domain, the NIST Framework calls 
for developing systems that are explainable, privacy-enhanced 
and subject to meaningful human supervision [11]; surveys of 
explainable AI stress the importance of having an explanation 
accompany any prediction affecting a human [7]. These criteria 
are applied by EngageIQ as requirements rather than options. 

D.  Anomaly Detection 

There  is  extensive  literature  on  anomaly  detection  tech-
niques,  surveyed by  Chandola  et  al.  [4].  Related  to our  case, 
HiPAL  is  an  anomaly  detector  predicting  physician  burnout 
based on activity logs from electronic health records instead of 

content  of  clinical  practice  [14],  which  supports  our  premise 
that  behavioral  metadata  has  burnout-related  signal.  Model-
based approaches like Isolation Forest [16] or LSTM autoen-
coders [15] are natural candidates to detect anomalies in time-
series.  With  short  and  noisy  time  series,  simple  statistical 
approaches are often more interpretable than machine learning 
methods. EngageIQ uses rolling mean and standard deviation 
for anomaly detection in the current prototype and leaves the 
sequence models as future work once there is enough history 
available. 

III.  PROBLEM  STATEMENT  AND  DESIGN  GOALS 

Given  weekly  work  metadata  for  a  number  of  employees, 
estimate  whether  each  person’s  current  pattern  of  work  and 
deliveries is healthy, drifting or at risk, provide an explanation 
and  route  the  case  to  a  human.  We  formulated  five  design 
goals before implementing the system. 
Privacy  first.  Use  only  metadata  such  as  durations,  counts, 
timestamps, and status fields. Do not process message content, 
keystrokes, screenshots, webcam images, or browsing history. 
Output  vs  Activity.  Give  preference  to  delivered  work  over 
activity to avoid punishing quiet yet productive individuals. 
Change over Level. Shift from a person’s own baseline is more 
valuable  than  a  single  low  value.  Explainability.  Each  score 
should  have  an  explanation  attached  to  it  (index  values  and, 
in full design, the underlying features that produced it). 
Human decision.  The system generates a signal and provides 
an explanation. It never recommends discipline or an automatic 
employment decision. 

IV.  SYSTEM  ARCHITECTURE 

The  EngageIQ  architecture  is  structured  into  a  pipeline  as 
seen  in  Fig.  1.  There  is  a  flow  of  information  from  other 
tools through the ingestion stage, privacy filtering process and 
two  layers  of  features  into  scoring,  trend  analysis  and  risk 
categorization to role-based dashboards. 

A.  Data Sources and Ingestion 

The  Signal  Layer  gets  data  from  calendar  and  communi-
cation tools (Google Calendar, Outlook, Slack and Microsoft 
Teams).  The  Output  Layer  gets  data  from  task  tracker  (Jira 
or  Asana),  version  control  system  (GitHub  or  GitLab),  and 
optionally  self-reported  effort.  Connectors  authenticate  with 
OAuth,  pull  data  on  a  schedule  and  sync  incrementally.  In 
the  current  prototype,  ingestion  is  a  validated  CSV  upload 
endpoint  carrying  the  same  normalised  feature  columns  that 
would be produced by the connectors, allowing the rest of the 
pipeline modules to be developed and tested in absence of live 
credentials.

missing  data  and  outliers,  normalises  every  feature  into  the 
range  [0,  1]  and  drops  any  data  that  is  not  on  the  approved 
metadata  list.  Raw  extracts  are  supposed  to  be  stored  in 
object  storage,  with  the  processed  features  and  scores  stored

C.  Scoring, Trend and Risk Modules 

Scoring  module  calculates  EHS,  OPI  and  the  fused  PACE 
score. The trend module compares the current scores against a 
rolling baseline. The risk classifier assigns one of three bands, 
with  the  score  and  index  values  being  stored  in  the  record. 
They are described in Section V. 

D.  Application Layer and Human Review 

Application layer exposes REST endpoints and a dashboard 
with  views  for  employees,  managers,  HR  and  leadership. 
Alerts  can  be  delivered by  email  or  in-app  notifications. The 
review panel allows a manager to open the case, add context, 
comment on the reasoning and override the signal so that the 
false  positives  are  corrected  and  the  reasoning  is  saved.  An 
audit  trail  and  role-based  access  control  are  the  privacy  and 
governance component of the modules.

B.  Output Layer: Output Productivity Index (OPI) 

From  task-tracker  and  repository  metadata  we  calculate 
completion  rate  c,  sprint  velocity  v,  deadline  adherence  d, 
code  activity  g  (commits and pull requests) and rework ratio 
w (reopened or revised issues per total issues). Rework is the 
only component that is negative for the person: 

OPI = 0.25c + 0.25v + 0.20d + 0.20g − 0.10w 

(2) 

and  the  resulting  value  is  clipped  to  [0,  1].  According  to 
the  design,  each  indicator  is  normalised  using  role-specific 
baselines so that, for instance, engineers’ commits and sales-
people’s  CRM  activities  are  not  comparable  under  the  same 
scale. Baselines are not yet implemented in the prototype and 
are listed as future work. 

C.  PACE Fusion 

V.  METHODOLOGY 

The final score is the weighted combination of two indices: 

A.   Signal Layer: Engagement and Work-Pattern Health (EHS) 

For  each  person  and  period  four  indicators  are  calculated 
based on calendar and chat metadata and normalised to [0, 1]; 
the greater the value, the heavier/less healthy the load: meeting 
load  m  (hours  in  meetings  divided  by  hours  worked),  focus 
gap  f  (one  minus  longest  uninterrupted  block  as  the  fraction 
of working hours), response latency r, and after-hours ratio a 

PACE = 0.4 × EHS + 0.6 × OPI 

(3) 

For example, a person with EHS = 0.50 and OPI = 0.80 will 
have PACE = 0.20 + 0.48 = 0.68. The heavier weight of OPI 
is  not  only  a  matter  of  statistics  but  also  ethics:  someone 
who communicates less frequently but always delivers on time 
should not fall into a risk band for that reason. 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
D.  Trend and Anomaly Detection 

Individual  scores  may  be  misleading,  hence  the  trend  de-
tection module takes into account the history of the particular 
individual.  Over  the  sliding  window  of  the  past  six  periods, 
the mean  µ and standard deviation  σ are calculated using the 
first five periods, and the deviation of the current period score 
s is 

z = 

µ − s 

σ 

(4) 

in such a way that positive  z indicates that the particular em-
ployee is doing worse than usual. If the deviation  z  surpasses 
a  certain  threshold  k  (set  to  1.5  by  default)  the  period  is 
considered  anomalous.  Further,  depending  on  whether or  not 
the  previous  period  exceeded  the  threshold,  the  situation  is 
called persistent deterioration or sudden change, respectively. 
The module is capable of detecting combined patterns which 
are of interest for management, such as deterioration of work-
pattern  health  without  changes  in  output,  or  deterioration  of 
both  layers  simultaneously.  The  prototype’s  dashboard  has  a 
simplified  client-side  implementation  that  detects  anomalies 
relative  to  the  past  history  over  the  past  four  weeks;  the 
entire  rolling-baseline  procedure  is  specified  and  waiting  to 
be integrated into the service. 

E.  Risk Classification 

The PACE score is classified into three risk bands according 
to  Table  II.  The  classifier  takes  the  flag  about  persistent 
deterioration provided by the trend module, and in case of its 
being set, escalates the band of a record by one regardless of 
the actual score. In the prototype the explanation that is stored 
along  with  the  band  is  the  breakdown  of  the  EHS  and  OPI 
scores;  the  plain-language  explanations  like  "output  is  stable 
while  work-pattern  health  has  deteriorated  and  meeting  load 
has increased" are part of the design and are demonstrated in 
simulator  scenarios.  The  flag  is  not  yet  passed  in  the  upload 
endpoint,  as  the  trend  procedure  is  not  yet  integrated  into 
the  prototype.  These  risk  bands  are  the  presentation-layer 
thresholds  and  require  calibration  using  real  organisational 
data to be used in production.

B.  API 

There  are  two  key  endpoints  for  the  process  flow.  POST 
request to upload endpoint uploads the CSV file, makes sure 
there is all twelve columns, creates employees in the database 
for  the  first  time,  calculates  EHS,  OPI,  PACE  and  risk  band 
for  each  record,  saves  everything  and  returns  the  summary. 
GET request to scores endpoint returns all saved scores along 
with the name, team and breakdown for the dashboard. 

A.  Technology Stack 

C.  Dashboard 

Back-end is a Python FastAPI application using Pandas for 
parsing/validation and SQLAlchemy for persistence, with two 
tables:  the  employees  table  and  the  score  records  table  that 
keeps the EHS, OPI, PACE score, risk band, explanation and 
timestamp. Front-end is a React 18 and TypeScript single-page 
application  built  on  top  of  Vite,  React  Router  and  Recharts 
with  a  possibility  to  switch  between  light  and  dark  themes. 
The technology stack consists of Apache Airflow for scheduled 
ingestion, PostgreSQL and S3 storage, OAuth 2.0 and JWT for 
authentication,  Docker  and  GitHub  Actions  for  deployment. 
All  production  components  are  considered  in  the  application 
architecture, but not all of them are actually connected in the 
prototype yet. 

The  dashboard  is  composed  of  four  main  sections.  Orga-
nization  dashboard  (Fig.  2)  shows  headcount,  mean  PACE, 
EHS  and  OPI,  as  well  as  distribution  of  people  across  risk 
bands.  Team  and  individual  dashboards  show  two  lenses  for 
each  person  side-by-side,  including  sparklines  and  particular 
features  that  moved.  Review  queue  and  review  panel  imple-
ment  the  human-in-the-loop  step.  The  PACE  simulator  (Fig. 
3)  allows  a  user  to  select  a  scenario  ("quiet  collaborator" 
or "collaboration slipping"), tweak weekly feature values and 
observe the resulting score and multi-week trend change. The 
simulator is mainly a transparency tool, as it allows employees 
and managers to see precisely how readings are turned into a 
score. 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
C.  Discussion 

These  results  prove  that  the  proposed  pipeline  works  as 
intended:  it  separates  the  three  synthetic  profiles  cleanly, 
generates  scores  within  the  expected  range  and  persists  EHS 
and  OPI  breakdown  with  each  record.  They  should  not  be 
taken as proof of effectiveness of PACE to detect real burnout 
since  the  data  has  been  generated  from  archetypes,  and  con-
sequently, the EHS and OPI are  highly correlated (r = 0.96). 
In  other  words,  the  separation  looks  easier  than  it  would  be 
in  real  teams  where  an  employee  could  be  healthy  in  terms 
of  work  patterns  and  have  poor  output  and  vice  versa.  The 
most  interesting  scenarios  for  PACE  are  those  when  the  two 
lenses  diverge,  and  such  cases  are  represented  by  simulator 
scenarios  "quiet  collaborator"  and  "collaboration  slipping". 
A  proper  validation  should  be  done  with  longitudinal  real 
data,  comparison  against  a  validated  burnout  instrument  and 
calculation of false-positive rate using manager feedback. 

VIII.  RESPONSIBLE  AI,  PRIVACY  AND  ETHICS 

Data  minimisation.  The  ingestion  schema  includes  numeric 
and  categorical  metadata  only.  Message  bodies,  attachments, 
screen content and personal browsing is excluded intentionally, 
and the processing step discards fields which are not included 
in the approved list. 
Human  oversight.  The  classifier  returns  a  band  and  an  ex-
planation,  but  no  decision  itself.  The  manager  or  HR  can 
comment  or  override  each  case,  and  the  reasoning  is  kept  in 
the  log.  This  aligns  with  the  principle  that  analytics  should 
facilitate discussion and leave the decision to the person with 
the context. 
Fairness.  The  factor  of  0.6  in  the  output  gives  less  weight  to 
introverts  or people  who  work  asynchronously.  Role-specific 
baselines  need  to  be  created  so  that  employees  from  various 
positions are not being compared by the same standard. Self-
comparison  against  one’s  own  baseline  rather  than  against 
peers limits the possibilities for unfair ranking. 
Transparency and consent. Employees need to be aware of the 
information that is being collected and of how scores are calcu-
lated. The simulator and employee-level views are supposed to 
make the approach auditable. Governance module will include 
role-based access control,  audit log and consent management 
to meet GDPR and SOC 2 compliance requirements. 
Misuse  risk.  Every  score  could  be  misused,  for  example,  as 
the basis for ranking employees during performance reviews. 
Potential mitigations involve showing trends rather than league 
tables,  limiting  the  audience  for  individual-level  data  and 
explicitly stating that the score is a trigger for assistance rather 
than an indication of value in the interface.

VII.  RESULTS  AND  DISCUSSION 

A.  Experimental Setup 

Since real employee metadata cannot be used due to privacy 
concerns  and  lack  of  access,  the  prototype  is  tested  on  a 
synthetically  generated  dataset  of  50  employees  divided  into 
seven  teams.  Each  synthetic  person  belongs  to  one  of  three 
behavioural  profiles  (healthy,  strained  or  at-risk)  and  has 
feature  values  sampled  from range  characteristic  to  a  profile. 
The  upload  endpoint processed  all  50  rows  and  persisted  the 
scores. All the graphs below have been generated by invoking 
the scoring functions directly on the same file. 

B.  Score Distribution 

On average the employees in the organisation had an EHS of 
0.60, an OPI of 0.47 and a PACE score of 0.52. The classifier 
classified 15 employees as Healthy, 25 as Moderate and 10 as 
At-Risk. Table III shows mean indices in each band and Fig. 3 
shows each individual along with the boundaries of the bands 
as per Eq. (3). Team averages vary from 0.68 for Data & ML 
to 0.41 for Growth Marketing. 

IX.  LIMITATIONS  AND  FUTURE  WORK 

There  are  certain  limitations  in  this  study.  First  of  all,  the 
evaluation  of  the  system  is  based  solely  on  the  synthetic 
data.  Second,  the  ingestion  process  is  implemented  through 
importing a CSV file, while the live connectors for calendars, 
chats,  trackers,  and  repositories  have  not  been  implemented 
yet. Third, the rolling-baseline anomaly detection method and 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
baseline  values  per  role  are  specified,  however,  the  imple-
mentation  is  yet  to  be  developed.  Fourth,  the  weights  and 
thresholds  values  are  expert-defined  and  uncalibrated.  Fifth, 
authentication,  audit  logging  and  the  notification  service  are 
at the design stage. 

Planned  future  steps  include:  connecting  actual  APIs 
through scheduled jobs; running a pilot study with consenting 
volunteers and a validated burnout questionnaire as the ground 
truth;  learning  role-specific  baselines;  using  Isolation  Forest 
[16]  or  an  LSTM  autoencoder  [15]  in  place  of  deviation 
test  after  collecting  sufficient  history  while  preserving  the 
requirement  for  explanation;  and  conducting  a  fairness  audit 
to ensure that there are no score differences by role, location, 
and work schedule patterns.

EngageIQ demonstrates that it is possible to provide organi-
zations with an early warning perspective on engagement and 
burnout risk assessment without analyzing content and screen 
activity.  Using  healthy  work-patterns  along  with  generated 
output,  favoring  output  in  the  weighing  process,  performing 
intra-individual  comparisons,  and  sending  all  signals  to  a 
human reviewer are some ways the PACE framework attempts 
to  ensure  that  the  analytics  remain  supportive  rather  than 
punitive.  The  prototype  demonstrates  the  implementation  of 
scoring, classification and dashboard layers on synthetic data. 
The following phase would be validation of the prototype on 
real consented data and evaluation of the value of the signals 
provided  to  managers  as  an  aid  to  supporting  their  teams  in 
an early stage.
